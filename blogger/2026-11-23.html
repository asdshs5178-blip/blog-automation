K-ETA or e-Arrival Card for Korea: Who Needs Which Form and What Changes in 2027

Short answer: Visitors from visa-free countries normally need a K-ETA (Korea Electronic Travel Authorization) before boarding, but nationals of about two dozen countries and regions, including the US, UK, Japan, Canada and Australia, are temporarily exempt until December 31, 2026 (Korea time). If you do not hold a K-ETA, you fill out the free e-Arrival Card online within 3 days before you land. What happens from January 1, 2027 has not been announced as of this writing, so anyone travelling early next year should plan for both possibilities. Below is how the two forms work, what they cost, and the mistakes I see most often in questions from visitors.

The two forms, side by side

People often mix these up because they both involve typing passport details into a government website. They are separate systems run by the Ministry of Justice, and most short-term visitors only need one of them.

The simplest way to think about it: a K-ETA replaces the arrival card. A person who has received K-ETA approval is exempt from filling out an arrival card at entry. If you do not have a K-ETA, you file the card.

Who needs a K-ETA right now (through December 31, 2026)

The Ministry of Justice has suspended the K-ETA requirement for a group of countries and regions since April 1, 2023 to support tourism, and the latest notice extends the temporary exemption by one more year, through December 31, 2026, Korea Standard Time. Korean embassy notices list these 22 countries and regions:

  • Asia: Japan, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, Macao
  • Americas: United States (including Guam), Canada
  • Europe: United Kingdom, Germany, France, Italy, Netherlands, Spain, Poland, Sweden, Finland, Norway, Belgium, Denmark, Austria
  • Oceania: Australia, New Zealand

In addition, the Korean Embassy in Switzerland states that Switzerland and Liechtenstein are temporarily exempted from the K-ETA requirement until 31 December 2026, which is why some sources count 24. The final word is the K-ETA portal itself: when a national of an exempt country selects their nationality during the K-ETA application, a "K-ETA exempt" pop-up appears, and you can stop there.

Separately from nationality, ages 17 years old and under, and 65 years old and above, have been exempted since July 3, 2023. Visa holders and registered foreign residents do not use K-ETA either.

Visa-free nationals who are not on the list above, for example from Thailand, Malaysia or most of Latin America, still need an approved K-ETA before the airline will issue a boarding pass. Being visa-free and being K-ETA-exempt are two different things.

What happens after December 31, 2026

This is the part I cannot answer with certainty. The exemption has a fixed end date written into the official notice. It has been extended twice before, both times in December: the extension to 2025 was announced by the Ministry of Justice on December 11 through the K-ETA website notice board in 2024, and the current exemption runs to December 31, 2026 under an extension announced on December 23, 2025. As of November 23, 2026, I could not find any official notice from the Ministry of Justice about 2027, in Korean or English; a Korean travel-industry report two days ago still cites only the 2025 notice. Several travel sites state as fact that K-ETA "returns" on January 1, 2027. That is what the current notice implies if nothing changes, but it is not a confirmed announcement.

If you are arriving in January or February 2027, you have two reasonable options:

  1. Apply for a K-ETA now anyway. Exempt nationals may still apply voluntarily. Nationals of exempt countries who want the benefits of a K-ETA, such as skipping the arrival card, may apply, and the application fee is charged. It stays valid for 3 years regardless of what is decided, but a K-ETA already issued can be used until its expiry date and is not refunded if the exemption is later extended.
  2. Wait and check the K-ETA notice board in late December. If an extension is posted, you travel on your passport alone plus the e-Arrival Card. If not, apply at least 72 hours before your flight, and give it more margin around the New Year, when processing volume is likely to rise.

If your dates are later in 2027 and flexible, waiting costs nothing. If a missed flight would be expensive, 10,000 won is cheap insurance. I will update this post when an official notice appears; the 2027 public holiday list is useful alongside it if you are picking dates.

How to apply for a K-ETA

Use only www.k-eta.go.kr or the official K-ETA mobile app. Look-alike sites charge many times the real fee and sometimes collect passport data; a page asking for 50 dollars or promising "express approval" is not the government.

  1. Prepare a valid passport, a credit card for the fee, an email address and a recent profile photo, plus your accommodation address in Korea.
  2. Open the site or app, choose Apply, agree to the terms, and enter or scan your passport details. If your nationality is exempt, a pop-up tells you here and you can stop.
  3. Enter your trip details and answer the questions about purpose of visit, occupation and address in Korea. Typos in the passport number or name are the most common reason for trouble at check-in, so compare field by field.
  4. Pay 10,000 won per person. The fee is 10,000 KRW per person, additional fees not included, so expect a small card charge on top. Note that non-members can only file individual applications; group bulk applications are limited to registered travel agencies and medical institutions and only on PC.
  5. Wait for the result. Results are sent by email within 72 hours of applying in normal conditions, but Korean missions warn that screening can take more than 72 hours depending on application volume, so apply with plenty of margin. You can apply at any time, but at least 72 hours before check-in for your flight or ship to Korea.
  6. Keep the approval email or a screenshot. Airlines check K-ETA status electronically, but having it on your phone saves time if a system check fails.

Approval is tied to the passport you applied with. Validity is 3 years from the approval date, with unlimited entries within that period. A new passport means a new application, and a K-ETA is not a guarantee of entry; the immigration officer makes the final decision at the counter.

The e-Arrival Card: who, when and how

Since February 24, 2025 (Korea time), the e-Arrival Card system lets you fill in and submit the arrival card online in advance instead of handing a paper form to the immigration officer. It is free, and a website asking for payment is not the official service. One caution: the launch notice said paper arrival cards would remain available alongside the online form only through December 2025. I could not confirm from an official source whether paper cards are still handed out in 2026, so plan to file online.

  • Who submits it: in principle, every foreign national entering Korea is required to submit an arrival card; the launch notice describes the same group as the paper card, that is, foreigners on short-term visas of 90 days or less and long-term visa holders who have not completed foreigner registration. Exceptions include foreigners who have completed registration in Korea (including permanent residents), holders of a valid K-ETA, group tourists on group e-visas, visa-free transit entrants and airline crew. You can check whether you need to file using the "e-Arrival card navigator" on the site.
  • When: you can submit the form up to 3 days before your arrival in Korea (KST), and if you do not arrive within 72 hours, the card is no longer valid. It is used up once you pass immigration, so you file again for every new entry.
  • What you need: passport, travel details, the address of where you are staying, and an email address. After you submit, you get a submission number, and you can use that number or verify through your email to find your card and make changes if needed.
  • Nothing to print: you can download or capture the confirmation on the site, and immigration inspection proceeds normally even without a printed confirmation.

Practical tip: book your first night before you fly so you have a real address to type in, and file the card the day before departure rather than at the gate, where airport Wi-Fi and time are both short.

Customs: no form if you have nothing to declare

The third form people remember from older trips, the customs declaration, is no longer required for most visitors. From May 1, 2023, travellers with nothing to declare to customs on entry no longer need to fill in a traveller's declaration form. Travellers and crew without declarable items simply enter through the "Nothing to Declare" channel without a form. You fill in a declaration (paper or in the Korea Customs Service mobile app) only if you carry goods above the US$800 duty-free allowance, foreign currency above US$10,000, or items subject to quarantine such as jerky, ham and fruit. Firearms, drugs and commercial samples also require declaration, and the government says it will strictly crack down on dishonest declarations and on the import of illegal items such as drugs and firearms. Details are on the Korea Customs Service site.

Once you are through customs, the next step is getting into the city; see Incheon Airport to Seoul by AREX, bus or taxi.

Common mistakes

  • Paying a third-party site for a K-ETA. The real fee is 10,000 won. Anything far above that is a reseller or worse.
  • Assuming the exemption covers 2027. It ends on December 31, 2026 unless a new notice is posted. Check the K-ETA notice board before any January trip.
  • Letting the e-Arrival Card lapse. The site only opens the 3-day window before your arrival date, and a filed card is void if you have not entered within 72 hours. If your flight is rebooked, check it and refile.
  • Submitting both. A valid K-ETA already replaces the arrival card; a second form just wastes time.
  • Using an old K-ETA with a new passport. The approval dies with the passport it was issued on.
  • Mistyping the passport number. Airlines match the K-ETA to the passport at check-in; one wrong digit can mean denied boarding and a fresh application and fee.
  • Forgetting the accommodation address. Both forms ask for it, and "hotel in Seoul" is not accepted.
