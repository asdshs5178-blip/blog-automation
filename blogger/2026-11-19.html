Emergency Numbers in Korea: 112, 119, 1330 and How to Find a Pharmacy or Doctor (2026)

Short answer: In Korea, dial 112 for police, 119 for fire, ambulance or rescue, and 1330 for the Korea Tourism Organization's travel helpline, which can interpret for you in English, Japanese and Chinese. All three work from any phone without an area code. If the problem is smaller than an emergency, this post also covers how to find a pharmacy that is open late, which basic medicines convenience stores can sell, and who to call when you need a doctor who speaks your language.

The three numbers to remember

Korea splits emergency calls by type rather than using a single number. The table below is what I would save in my phone before arriving.

From a foreign phone number on roaming, 1330 can be reached as +82-2-1330. For 112 and 119, dialing the three digits from a Korean SIM or a roaming phone connected to a Korean network is the normal way.

112: police

112 is the number for anything involving a crime or personal safety. The "112 foreign-language interpretation service", which lets tourists, students and multicultural families report to 112 through an interpreter, runs 365 days a year, 24 hours a day. The Korean National Police Agency had hired four English and Chinese interpreters in June 2023 to cover weekdays from 9am to 9pm, then added twelve more interpreters for the same two languages and expanded the service to 365 days, 24 hours from March 18, 2024. The interpreters work from a 112 foreign-language interpretation center that opened at the Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency.

In practice this means: dial 112, say "English" clearly, and wait. A foreign caller is put on a three-way call with the 112 interpreter and the dispatcher. Have your location ready. If you do not know the address, read out the name of the nearest subway station exit, a shop sign, or the small blue-and-white address plates on buildings. The police have said they plan to extend interpretation to Japanese, Vietnamese and other languages, but as of this writing I could only confirm English and Chinese as in-house languages.

119: fire, ambulance and rescue

119 handles fire, medical emergencies, ambulances, and rescue in the mountains or at sea. It is operated by the fire service, not the police, so a car accident with injuries is 119 and a stolen wallet is 112. If you are unsure, call either one; they transfer calls between each other.

Language support at 119 is less uniform than at 112, because regional fire headquarters run their own arrangements. North Jeolla's fire headquarters has 117 volunteer interpreters covering 14 languages (Vietnamese, Chinese, English, Cambodian, Uzbek, Japanese, Mongolian, Nepali, Thai, Burmese, Indonesian, Russian, Arabic and Sinhala); when a foreign caller reports, the 119 control room, the caller and the interpreter are connected in a three-way call. South Chungcheong expanded its 119 interpretation from 13 to 15 languages in June 2026, adding Mongolian and Sinhala, and also runs an AI-based multilingual system that gives 24-hour coverage at night and handles Indonesian, German, Italian and French, which its volunteers do not. South Gyeongsang's fire headquarters began an AI system in November 2026 that interprets foreign-language 119 calls in real time in English, Japanese, Chinese and Vietnamese. South Jeolla connects the 119 operator, the caller and an interpretation agency on a three-way call for English, Chinese, Japanese, Vietnamese, Thai and Russian. Other regions have their own setups, so do not assume a specific language will be available immediately.

If you cannot get through in your language, call 1330 and ask them to connect you: besides tourist information, 1330 provides three-way interpretation support for public lines such as 112, 119 and 1339. Speak slowly, give your location first, and keep the line open.

1330: not an emergency, but you need help

1330 is the number most travelers will actually use. The 1330 Korea Travel Helpline is a phone and real-time chat service provided by the Korea Tourism Organization to guide domestic and international tourists with various travel information. Services are tour information, tourism interpretation, reception of tourist complaints, and connection to the Tourist Police, in Korean, English, Chinese, Japanese, Russian, Vietnamese, Thai, Malay and Indonesian. It is a public facility and does not charge extra fees; the call is also toll-free through the "Visit Korea" app while in a Wi-Fi area.

One caution on hours. KTO's own English page says the helpline is open 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, with Russian, Vietnamese, Thai and Malay limited to 08:00-19:00. However, several regional tourism offices now quote newer KTO material giving 365 days, 17 hours (07:00-24:00). I could not resolve which is current, so plan on 1330 being reliable from 7am to midnight, and go straight to 112 or 119 for anything urgent in the small hours. Typical uses: a taxi driver cannot understand your destination, a pharmacist needs to ask about your symptoms, or you want to know whether a bus line runs on a holiday. You hand the phone over and let the operator talk to the other person.

Finding a pharmacy, by time of day

Pharmacies (약국, yakguk) are everywhere near subway stations and clinics, and most keep roughly office-plus-evening hours on weekdays. The gap is late night, Sundays and public holidays. Korea fills that gap with three layers.

  1. Daytime on Sundays and holidays: duty pharmacies. The Korean Pharmaceutical Association's Pharm114 "holiday-keeper pharmacy" site lists pharmacies scheduled to open on holidays, searchable by region and date. The site is in Korean, which works with a browser's translate function. The address is www.pharm114.or.kr.
  2. 10pm to 1am: public late-night pharmacies. Seoul runs public late-night pharmacies from 10pm to 1am for residents who suddenly need medicine late at night; in 2026 there are 39 of them, 28 open 365 days a year and 11 open only on certain days of the week. The city's ordinance was revised in July 2025 to fix the hours at 10pm to 1am. Locations can be found on the Seoul city website or by searching "공공심야약국" on Naver. Phone before you walk over, especially to one of the day-of-week pharmacies. Other cities run similar schemes with their own hours.
  3. Anytime: the E-Gen emergency medical portal. E-Gen, run by the National Emergency Medical Center, shows nearby emergency rooms, hospitals, pharmacies and AEDs. Opening hours can change, so confirm with the institution before going. Website: www.e-gen.or.kr.

If you will be in Korea over a long holiday, this matters more than usual, because many clinics and pharmacies close for several days at once. The dates are in my post on Korea's 2027 public holidays and what closes.

What a convenience store can sell you

When every pharmacy is shut, 24-hour convenience stores sell a short, fixed list of "safe over-the-counter medicines" (안전상비의약품). When the system started in 2012 the law allowed up to 20 items, but only 13 were designated, and two of those have since been discontinued, so 11 are actually on sale. The two that disappeared are the 80 mg children's Tylenol tablet and the 160 mg Tylenol tablet, whose approvals were withdrawn. What remains: Tylenol 500 mg tablets, a children's acetaminophen suspension and a children's ibuprofen syrup; two combination cold medicines (Pancol A and Panpyrin); four digestive aids (two Bearse and two Festal products); and two pain-relief patches.

This may change soon. The Ministry of Health and Welfare plans to gather expert and stakeholder input, decide on additional product categories, and revise the designation notice in December 2026, expanding the list within the legal ceiling of 20 items; antacids, anti-diarrheals and artificial tears are mentioned as candidates. The Korean Pharmaceutical Association opposes the expansion, citing misuse and side-effect risks. Until a new list is actually published, assume the 11 above. Note what is not on it: nothing for diarrhea, no antihistamines, no antacids, no motion-sickness tablets. Bring those from home or buy them at a pharmacy during the day. Stores may only sell one pack of each item at a time and may not sell to children under 12. Ask staff for "sangbiyak" (상비약) if you cannot find the shelf; it is often behind or beside the counter.

When you need to see a doctor

For a non-emergency illness, the normal route is a neighborhood clinic (의원) during the day. Payment is at the counter after the visit, and you will usually be handed a prescription to take to a pharmacy next door. English availability varies a lot by clinic, which is where the next number helps.

The Medical Korea Information Center provides consultation and information on medical services in Korea in English, Chinese, Japanese and Russian, through its Medical Call line at +82-2-1577-7129, open 09:00 to 18:00 daily (Korea time). It offers medical counseling, interpretation and legal consultations for foreign patients, including counseling on medical disputes and help filing applications with the Korea Medical Dispute Mediation and Arbitration Agency. There are two walk-in desks: one on floor B2 of the Airport Railroad concourse at Seoul Station, open 09:00 to 18:00 daily in English, Chinese, Japanese and Korean, and one at Gate 7 on the first floor of Incheon Airport Terminal 1, open 09:00 to 21:00 daily in English, Chinese, Japanese, Russian and Korean. I have seen the Terminal 1 desk in passing but have not used the service myself, so treat this as directory information rather than a review. The official page is medicalkorea.or.kr.

Larger hospitals in Seoul and other big cities have international clinics with coordinators, but they are not emergency rooms and generally work on appointments. For a true emergency, go to the nearest ER or call 119; sort out language afterwards. Bring your passport; credit cards are accepted at hospitals.

By situation, and mistakes to avoid

  • Lost passport or theft: 112 first for a police report, which your embassy will ask for. 1330 can help you explain if the interpreter is slow to join.
  • Chest pain, breathing trouble, serious injury: 119 immediately. Do not spend time searching E-Gen; that tool is for when you can travel on your own.
  • Fever or stomach upset at 11pm: look up a public late-night pharmacy and call it before walking over; if none is open, a convenience store covers fever, colds and indigestion only.
  • Need a doctor who speaks English next week: call 1577-7129 between 9am and 6pm, or ask 1330 for a nearby option.
  • Mistake: calling 911 or 999. These do not route to Korean services. Save 112 and 119.
  • Mistake: assuming a pharmacy is open because the map app says so. Hours on maps are user-edited and often wrong on holidays. Phone first.
  • Mistake: expecting ibuprofen tablets at a convenience store. Only the children's syrup form is on the list; adult tablets are pharmacy-only.
