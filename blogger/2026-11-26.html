Fine Dust in Korea: PM2.5 Grades, Alerts and When to Wear a KF94 Mask (2026–27)

Short answer: Korea reports air quality in four grades. For PM2.5, the particles most people mean when they say "fine dust", a daily average of 0–15 µg/m³ is Good, 16–35 is Moderate, 36–75 is Bad and 76 or more is Very Bad. A separate alert system starts when hourly readings stay high: a PM2.5 advisory when the hourly average reaches 75 µg/m³ for two hours and a warning at 150. The hazy stretch runs from December to March, which is why the eighth seasonal management period runs from 1 December 2026 to 31 March 2027. On a Bad day, most visitors shorten outdoor time and wear a KF80 or KF94 mask from a pharmacy or convenience store. Trips rarely need to be rearranged, but it helps to know what the numbers and phone alerts mean.

What "fine dust" means in Korea

Korean forecasts track two sizes of particle. Misemeonji (미세먼지) is PM10, particles 10 micrometres or smaller. Chomisemeonji (초미세먼지) is PM2.5, 2.5 micrometres or smaller, roughly one twentieth to one thirtieth the width of a hair. Weather apps, news tickers and station screens show both, and the grades for each have different cut-offs, so a "Moderate" PM10 reading is not the same number as a "Moderate" PM2.5 reading.

Concentrations are usually highest between late autumn and early spring, when air stagnates and particles from domestic sources and from outside the country build up. Summer and early autumn are generally cleaner. If you are visiting between December and March, a daily air quality check is worth adding to the morning routine, alongside the temperature. Our post on Korea in winter covers the cold side of the same season.

The four forecast grades

The grades below are the ones used by AirKorea, the national air quality site run by the Korea Environment Corporation under the Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment. They are 24-hour averages in micrograms per cubic metre.

Good (좋음)0–150–30No restrictions

AirKorea lists PM10 as Good 0–30, Moderate 31–80, Bad 81–150 and Very Bad 151 and above, with PM2.5 at 0–15, 16–35, 36–75 and 76 and above. When readings are Bad, sensitive groups, meaning pregnant women, infants, children, older people and people with cardiovascular or respiratory conditions, are told to avoid strenuous outdoor activity, and everyone else to reduce long or strenuous time outdoors. For a healthy adult, a Bad day usually means shorter walks and a mask, not a day stuck in the hotel. Note that the real-time reading on the same site can differ from the forecast grade, because one is an hourly value and the other is a predicted daily average.

Advisories, warnings and emergency reduction measures

Forecast grades are predictions. Alerts are triggered by what the monitoring stations actually measure, and they are issued by each metropolitan city or province on the basis of hourly average concentrations. There are two levels.

AlertPM2.5 triggerPM10 triggerLifted when
Advisory (주의보)Hourly average 75 µg/m³ or more for 2 hours150 µg/m³ or more for 2 hoursPM2.5 below 35 / PM10 below 100
Warning (경보)Hourly average 150 µg/m³ or more for 2 hours300 µg/m³ or more for 2 hoursDowngraded to advisory when PM2.5 drops below 75 / PM10 below 150

These thresholds come from the Enforcement Rules of the Clean Air Conservation Act: a PM10 advisory at 150 µg/m³ for two hours, lifted below 100; a PM10 warning at 300, downgraded below 150; a PM2.5 advisory at 75, lifted below 35; and a PM2.5 warning at 150, downgraded below 75.

A third mechanism, the emergency reduction measure (비상저감조치), is decided at 5 p.m. for the following day. It is issued by each city or province when any one of these conditions is met, and runs from 6 a.m. to 9 p.m. the next day: today's average is above 50 µg/m³ and tomorrow is forecast above 50; an advisory or warning was issued today and tomorrow is forecast above 50; or tomorrow is forecast above 75. The measures mostly target emissions: grade-5 vehicles are restricted, with a 100,000 won fine enforced by camera; public bodies run odd-even car rationing; and construction sites adjust or cut their hours by half or more. For a visitor the visible effect is small. Buses, trains and attractions operate as usual.

Yellow dust (황사) is a separate spring phenomenon handled by the Korea Meteorological Administration, which issues a yellow dust warning when the hourly PM10 concentration is expected to stay at 800 µg/m³ or more for two hours or longer. That level is rare, but on a yellow dust day PM10 climbs first, so in March and April it is worth looking at both numbers rather than only PM2.5.

The seasonal management period: 1 December 2026 to 31 March 2027

Every winter the government runs a seasonal management period (미세먼지 계절관리제) with stricter emission controls. The eighth plan was approved on 24 November 2026 at the 20th meeting of the Special Committee on Fine Dust, chaired by Prime Minister Han Seong-sook. The target is an average PM2.5 of 19 µg/m³ over the period, 11.6 percent below the 21.5 µg/m³ recorded during the seventh period. During the four months, grade-5 vehicles that have not had emission retrofits are barred from driving in Seoul, Incheon and Gyeonggi and in Busan, Daegu, Gwangju, Daejeon, Ulsan and Sejong, with a fine of 100,000 won per day if caught. This matters to owners of old diesel cars, not to tourists. The practical point for travellers is simply that this is the window when Bad days are most likely.

Phone alerts: what the Korean text message says

Korea sends cell-broadcast disaster messages to phones on its mobile networks. Under the national rules, a fine dust message is sent when a warning is issued or an emergency reduction measure is announced, and only in daytime hours from 6 a.m. to 9 p.m.; yellow dust warnings follow the same daytime rule. Fine dust falls under the "safety guidance" category, which uses the phone's normal notification setting; the maximum-volume alarm is reserved for civil defence alerts such as air raid warnings. The standard wording for an emergency reduction measure gives the hours and area, notes that grade-5 vehicles are restricted, and asks people to wear a health mask when going out and to use public transport. The text is in Korean, although alerts now include brief information in English on the type and severity of the alert. For a full translation, use the Emergency Ready App: the Ministry of the Interior and Safety provides disaster information through it in 22 languages, including English, Chinese and Japanese.

How to check air quality in English

[[PHOTO: AirKorea air quality monitoring station Seoul | A roadside air quality monitoring station of the type that feeds AirKorea's real-time data.]]

  1. Open the AirKorea English site at airkorea.or.kr/eng. It shows hourly readings by city and monitoring station, graded with the same cut-offs as the Korean grades above, plus the forecast for today and tomorrow.
  2. Forecasts are issued several times a day. The 5 p.m. bulletin is the one that adds a grade for the day after tomorrow, so an early check and a late-afternoon check can differ.
  3. For yellow dust in spring, the Korea Meteorological Administration's site, weather.go.kr, carries the warnings and PM10 observations.
  4. Naver's air quality display is based on AirKorea's public monitoring data combined with a forecast model, so it can differ from the measured value; most Korean weather apps work the same way and show a colour-coded grade next to the temperature.
  5. Set up the Emergency Ready App before a winter trip if you want the text alerts translated.

One caution: international air quality apps often convert Korean readings into the US AQI scale, where numbers run from 0 to 500. A US AQI of 100 is not 100 µg/m³. If a friend in Korea says "it's 40 today", they almost certainly mean the µg/m³ figure, which is a mild Bad day for PM2.5.

Masks: what KF80 and KF94 mean

Masks sold as health masks (보건용 마스크) in Korea are approved as quasi-drugs by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety and carry a KF (Korea Filter) rating. KF80 filters at least 80 percent of test particles averaging 0.6 µm; KF94 and KF99 filter at least 94 and 99 percent of particles averaging 0.4 µm. A higher number blocks more but can make breathing harder, so the ministry advises choosing a product to suit the dust level and your own breathing. KF80 is the usual pick for an ordinary Bad day; KF94 for Very Bad days or for people who want more protection.

  • Buy them at pharmacies, supermarkets and convenience stores. Warm-weather face coverings (방한대) with no filtering performance and unlicensed masks are sometimes sold as dust masks, so check the packet for the words 의약외품 (quasi-drug) and a KF80, KF94 or KF99 mark.
  • Do not wash a KF mask: washing deforms it and the filtering function is lost, and a used mask should not be reused because of possible dust and germ contamination.
  • Do not put tissue or a towel inside the mask; it reduces the seal and lowers the filtering effect.
  • Check the expiry date on the packaging.

What a Bad day looks like in practice

Expect a grey sky, poorer visibility from viewpoints, and more masks on the subway. Shops, restaurants, palaces and museums stay open. Under an advisory, kindergartens and elementary schools are asked to avoid outdoor classes and outdoor sports facilities run by public bodies restrict operation; under a warning, outdoor classes are stopped and those facilities close. Tourist sites do not close. Sensible adjustments are small: move a long hike or an open-air viewpoint to a cleaner day, and swap in indoor options such as museums, underground shopping streets or a jjimjilbang. If your eyes sting or you develop a cough, a pharmacist can help; our guide to pharmacies and emergency numbers explains how to find one. Anyone with asthma or a heart condition should follow their own doctor's advice rather than the general public guidance.

Common mistakes

  • Reading a US AQI number as a Korean µg/m³ grade, or the other way round.
  • Assuming every grey morning is pollution. Winter fog and low cloud are common; check the reading before deciding.
  • Cancelling a day out over a Moderate forecast. Moderate is the normal state for much of the year.
  • Buying a cloth or warm-weather mask and expecting it to filter PM2.5.
  • Watching only PM2.5 in spring and missing a PM10 spike from yellow dust.
  • Expecting attractions or transport to close under an advisory. They do not.
