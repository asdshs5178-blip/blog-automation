Korea Winter Weather: How Cold Seoul Gets in Dec–Feb and What to Pack (2026)

Short answer: Winter in Korea is cold and dry. In Seoul the January average is around -2°C, with typical morning lows near -5°C and cold snaps that go well below -10°C. Busan and Jeju are noticeably milder. The practical issues for a visitor are not just the temperature but the short days (sunset before 5:30 pm in late December), occasional fine dust episodes, and the gap between heated indoor spaces and the street. Layers, a real winter coat, shoes with grip and a plan for checking the forecast cover most of it. Below are the official numbers and how to read the alerts you may receive on your phone.

How cold does it actually get?

The Korea Meteorological Administration (KMA) publishes 30-year climate normals, and the current set covers 1991–2020. January is the coldest month in most of the country. The table below uses KMA's January normals for major cities.

The monthly tables give January mean temperatures of Seoul -1.9, Gangneung 0.9, Gwangju 1.0, Busan 3.6, Daegu 1.1 and Jeju 6.1; January average highs of Seoul 2.1, Gangneung 5.3, Gwangju 5.7, Busan 8.2, Daegu 5.9 and Jeju 8.6; and January average lows of Seoul -5.5, Gangneung -2.7, Gwangju -2.7, Busan -0.1, Daegu -2.9 and Jeju 3.7. The next set of normals is not due until 2031.

Two things the averages hide. First, Seoul winters move in waves: a few very cold days with a strong northwesterly wind, then a few milder days. Seoul does see mornings at or below -12°C, the cold wave advisory threshold, in a typical winter. Second, the air is dry, so the cold feels sharp rather than damp, and lips and skin dry out quickly. December and February are usually a little milder than January, but still cold enough for a proper winter coat in the capital region and the inland cities.

For the daily forecast in English, use the KMA English site at kma.go.kr/neng. Its current-weather table lists air temperature, dew point and a wind chill temperature, and the Korean site and app show the same "feels like" value (체감온도) in winter.

Cold wave alerts: what the message on your phone means

Korea issues two levels of cold wave alert. The names you will see in news or on apps are "cold wave advisory" (한파주의보) and "cold wave warning" (한파경보). The thresholds are specific:

AlertIssued when (any one condition)
Cold wave advisory (한파주의보)Morning low of -12°C or below expected for 2 or more days; or morning low expected to drop 10°C or more from the previous day to 3°C or below and at least 3°C below normal; or sudden cold likely to cause serious damage
Cold wave warning (한파경보)Morning low of -15°C or below expected for 2 or more days; or a drop of 15°C or more to 3°C or below and at least 3°C below normal; or widespread serious damage expected

The advisory is issued between October and April when one of two conditions is met: the morning low is expected to fall 10°C or more from the previous day to 3°C or below while being at least 3°C below normal, or a morning low of -12°C or below is expected to last two days or more; it is also issued when a sudden drop in temperature is expected to cause serious damage. The warning uses -15°C for two or more days, or a drop of 15°C or more to 3°C or below and 3°C below normal, or serious damage expected over a wide area. When KMA set the -12°C threshold in 2010, it explained that -12°C corresponded to the coldest 5% of daily lows in Korea over the previous ten years, and that frozen pipes rise sharply when it persists. The -12°C and -15°C thresholds have not changed since 2010.

Government emergency text messages (재난문자) are sent in Korean. If you want them in another language, the Ministry of the Interior and Safety runs a free app for foreigners. The Emergency Ready App is the foreigner version of the government's main disaster and safety app, 안전디딤돌, and provides disaster alerts, action guides and embassy information as well as shelter locations. In a June 2026 overhaul the ministry expanded the supported languages from three (English, Chinese, Japanese) to 22, with the revamped service starting on 15 June. Install it before you land, and turn on push alerts and set your region in the settings menu; language and reception-area settings are separate, so changing the language alone does not finish the setup. For actual emergencies, 119 is fire and ambulance and 112 is police; the Korea Travel Hotline 1330 helps with interpretation.

Short days: plan outdoor sights for the middle of the day

Daylight is short. According to the Korea Astronomy and Space Science Institute (KASI), which published the figures on 24 December 2025, Seoul's sunset on 31 December was 5:23 pm and its sunrise on 1 January 2026 was 7:47 am. That gives you less than ten hours of daylight at the turn of the year. The published times are calculated for sea level, and sunrise comes earlier at higher elevations. Palaces, hiking trails and outdoor markets are best done between late morning and about 4 pm; check each site's winter closing time, since many close earlier than in summer. Evening plans work well indoors: night markets under cover, department store food halls, jjimjilbang (bathhouses) and cafes. You can look up exact times for any Korean city on the KASI site at astro.kasi.re.kr.

What to pack for Korea in winter

Indoor spaces in Korea are heated hard. Subway cars, buses, cafes and shops are often warm enough that you will want to open your coat within a minute. Most Korean homes and many guesthouses use ondol floor heating, so rooms are warm from the floor up. The result is that layering matters more than one very thick garment.

  • A windproof coat that covers your hips, ideally a long padded coat. In Seoul, long down jackets are what most locals wear from December through February.
  • Thin layers underneath: a thermal top, a sweater or fleece you can take off indoors.
  • Hat, gloves and a scarf or neck warmer. Wind on open streets, river paths and palace grounds is the part people underestimate.
  • Shoes with grip. Sidewalks and stairs ice over after snow, and many are stone or tile. Avoid smooth-soled shoes.
  • Warm socks without holes. You will take your shoes off in many places: hanok stays, some restaurants with floor seating, jjimjilbang and temple halls.
  • Lip balm and moisturizer. The air is dry outside and heating dries it further inside.
  • A reusable water bottle. Heated indoor air is dehydrating.

Things you do not need to bring: disposable hand and foot warmers (핫팩) are sold at every convenience store and at many subway station kiosks in winter, and KF-rated dust masks are sold at pharmacies and convenience stores.

Fine dust: what the colors on the air quality app mean

Winter and early spring are the seasons when fine dust episodes are most likely, because cold, stable air traps pollution. It does not happen every day, and many winter days have clear blue skies after a cold front passes. When it does happen, the national AirKorea service grades PM2.5 (fine particles) in four levels:

GradePM2.5 (µg/m³)
Good (좋음)0–15
Moderate (보통)16–35
Bad (나쁨)36–75
Very bad (매우나쁨)76 and above

AirKorea lists PM2.5 as Good 0–15, Moderate 16–35, Bad 36–75 and Very bad 76 and above, with the 24-hour environmental standard at 35 µg/m³. On "Bad" days many locals wear a KF80 or KF94 mask outdoors and keep windows closed; on "Very bad" days it is reasonable to move outdoor plans indoors. Real-time readings and the next-day forecast are at airkorea.or.kr/eng. One thing to watch: in its 2026 work plan published on 21 January, the Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment said it intends to lower the national PM2.5 environmental standard, currently an annual average of 15 µg/m³, and the specific figure will be fixed after expert debates and public hearings. The grade thresholds in the table are the ones in use today, but they may be revised once that process ends.

Getting around when it snows

Seoul does not get huge amounts of snow, but a few centimeters is enough to slow the city. On snow days the subway is the most reliable option because it is unaffected by road conditions. Buses run but slower, and taxi demand spikes in the evening. If you are arriving on a snowy day, the airport railroad is the predictable choice; see the Incheon Airport to Seoul guide for fares and times. Intercity trains (KTX) generally keep running in normal winter weather, while highway buses can be delayed. Flights to and from Jeju can be delayed or cancelled by strong winter winds and snow, so leave buffer time if you connect from Jeju to an international flight.

For paying on buses and subways, a transport card is easier than cash with gloves on; the options are compared in this post on T-money, Climate Card and WOWPASS.

Common mistakes

  1. Packing for the average, not the cold snap. A -2°C average means some mornings are -10°C or lower. Bring the warmer coat.
  2. One thick layer instead of several thin ones. You will overheat on the subway and freeze on the platform. Layers you can remove solve both.
  3. Booking everything outdoors after 4 pm. With sunset around 5:20 pm in late December, that leaves little time with light.
  4. Ignoring the "feels like" temperature. Wind is the difference between a fine walk along the Han River and a miserable one.
  5. Expecting traditional stays to be as warm as a hotel. Hanok rooms with ondol are warm on the floor, but older buildings can be drafty. Ask the host how heating works before you arrive.
  6. Not checking air quality. Thirty seconds on AirKorea in the morning tells you whether to grab a mask.
