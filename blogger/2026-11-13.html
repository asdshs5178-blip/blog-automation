Korea in Winter: How Cold It Gets, What to Pack and What Cold Wave Alerts Mean (Dec–Feb)

Short answer: Korean winters are cold, dry and bright. In Seoul, the January average is -1.9°C, with a typical morning low of -5.5°C and an afternoon high of 2.1°C, based on the Korea Meteorological Administration (KMA) climate normals for 1991–2020. Busan and Jeju are several degrees milder but still need a proper coat. Pack layers you can take off indoors, a windproof outer layer, and something for your ears and hands. Below are the official figures by city, a packing list, and a plain explanation of the cold wave and heavy snow alerts you may see on your phone.

How cold does Korea get in December, January and February?

The table uses KMA's 1991–2020 climate normals, the official 30-year averages the agency published on 25 March 2021. January is the coldest month everywhere on the mainland, and December and February sit a few degrees above it, so the figures below are for January: that is the month to plan around. Inland and northern areas (Seoul, Daejeon, Gangwon's interior) are colder than the south coast, and Jeju is the mildest of the major destinations.

The numbers come from KMA's own summary tables: January mean temperatures of -1.9 (Seoul), 0.9 (Gangneung), -1.0 (Daejeon), 3.6 (Busan), 1.1 (Daegu) and 6.1 (Jeju), with January average highs of 2.1, 5.3, 4.1, 8.2, 5.9 and 8.6, and January average lows of -5.5, -2.7, -5.5, -0.1, -2.9 and 3.7 respectively. A few things the averages hide. First, the gap between morning and afternoon is large: in Seoul the difference between the January average low and high is 7.6°C (my own subtraction from the KMA figures), so a walk at 8 a.m. and a walk at 2 p.m. feel like different days. Second, averages include mild spells. Cold snaps that push Seoul mornings below -10°C happen most winters, and this is exactly when the alerts described below are issued. Third, Busan is about 5.5°C milder than Seoul in January and Jeju City about 8°C milder (again my own calculation), but both are coastal and can feel colder than the number because of wind. For the whole December–February season, KMA's regional climate page gives Busan a winter mean of 5.0°C, with strong winds when the cold continental high expands.

You can check the normals for any station on the KMA data portal at data.kma.go.kr (Korean), and daily forecasts on weather.go.kr.

Short days and dry air

Daylight is short in midwinter. For the 2024–2025 New Year, the Korea Astronomy and Space Science Institute (KASI) listed Seoul's sunset on 31 December at 17:24 and sunrise on 1 January at 7:47, and for 1 January 2026 Seoul's sunrise was again given as 7:47 a.m. The times shift by only a minute or two from year to year. Plan outdoor sightseeing for roughly 9 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. and keep evenings for markets, restaurants and indoor places.

Winter is also the dry season. Rain and snow are infrequent compared with summer, skies are often clear, and indoor heating dries the air further. Lip balm, hand cream and a small bottle of moisturizer are more useful than an umbrella on most winter trips. Fine dust (미세먼지) episodes also occur in winter and early spring; the AirKorea site, run under the Ministry of Environment, shows current levels in English, and many travelers simply carry a KF94 mask for those days.

What to pack for Korea in winter

The main challenge is not the cold itself but the swing between outdoors and indoors. Subway cars, buses, cafes and shops are heated, often warmly, and many guesthouses and hanok stays use ondol (heated floors). A single very heavy coat with a thin shirt underneath leaves you sweating inside and cold outside. Layers solve this.

  • Base and mid layers: a thermal top (heat-tech style), a sweater or fleece, and a shirt you are comfortable wearing alone indoors.
  • Outer layer: a windproof, preferably knee-length, insulated coat or down jacket. Wind matters more than the thermometer in Seoul.
  • Head, hands, neck: a hat that covers your ears, gloves (touchscreen-compatible is handy for transit cards and maps), and a scarf or neck gaiter.
  • Footwear: shoes with grip and some insulation. Sidewalks can have icy patches after a snowfall, especially in the morning on side streets and hills.
  • Skin and lips: lip balm and moisturizer, as noted above.
  • Hot packs: you do not need to bring these. Disposable hand warmers are sold at convenience stores, drugstores and station kiosks all winter; you can buy them as needed.
  • For Busan, Jeju and the east coast: the same list, minus perhaps the thermal base layer in December. Do not skip the windproof coat.
  • For ski areas and Gangwon mountains: add a thermal base layer for your legs and warmer gloves. Mountain stations in Gangwon are several degrees colder than Seoul on the same day.

Coin lockers and storage counters at major stations and airports make it easy to shed a heavy layer before an indoor-heavy day. Indoor temperatures in Korea are a matter of preference and vary by building, so expect some places to feel too warm.

Cold wave and heavy snow alerts: what the terms mean

KMA issues two levels of special weather notice for cold: an advisory (주의보) and a warning (경보). The criteria below are from KMA's published standards, which for cold waves apply from October to April.

AlertKoreanMain criteria
Cold wave advisory한파주의보Morning low of -12°C or below expected for 2 or more days; or a drop of 10°C or more from the previous day to 3°C or below and at least 3°C below normal; or severe damage expected from a sudden cold spell
Cold wave warning한파경보Morning low of -15°C or below expected for 2 or more days; or a drop of 15°C or more from the previous day to 3°C or below and at least 3°C below normal; or widespread severe damage expected
Heavy snow advisory대설주의보5 cm or more of new snow expected in 24 hours
Heavy snow warning대설경보20 cm or more of new snow in 24 hours (30 cm in mountain areas)

In the official Korean wording, a cold wave advisory is issued between October and April when the morning low is expected to fall 10°C or more from the previous day to 3°C or below and 3°C below normal, when the morning low is expected to stay at -12°C or below for two or more days, or when serious damage is expected from a sudden cold spell; the warning uses 15°C and -15°C and widespread damage. For snow, the advisory is for 24-hour new snowfall of 5 cm or more and the warning for 20 cm or more, or 30 cm in mountain areas. Note that cold wave alerts still use actual air temperature, unlike heat alerts, which switched to "feels like" temperature in 2023.

In practice, a cold wave advisory in Seoul means mornings cold enough to be uncomfortable for more than a few minutes without a hat and gloves. A heavy snow advisory means slower buses and taxis, slippery pavements, and possible flight delays, but the subway and intercity trains usually keep running. KMA also calculates a winter wind-chill temperature and publishes it as part of its living weather information; it is not part of the alert criteria, but it is the number to look at before heading out.

Alerts on your phone

Korea sends government emergency text alerts (긴급재난문자) to phones in an affected area. They arrive in Korean, and since 28 February 2024 the Ministry of the Interior and Safety and KMA add an English label for the disaster type (and earthquake magnitude) to the two most serious tiers, the ones that come with an alarm tone. For fuller translations, the Ministry of the Interior and Safety runs the free Emergency Ready App, the foreigner-facing counterpart to its Korean-language 안전디딤돌 app; it delivers disaster information in 22 languages including English, Chinese, Japanese, Vietnamese and Thai and shows nearby shelters, emergency medical centers and embassy contacts. If you do not read Korean, install it before you arrive. In winter, look at the Korean text for 한파 (cold wave), 대설 (heavy snow) or 미세먼지 (fine dust).

Getting around in winter

Public transport is the easiest way to travel in cold weather because stations, trains and buses are heated and run on schedule in all but severe snow. If you are arriving in the cold months, the airport train is the most weather-proof option into the city; see Incheon Airport to Seoul: AREX Train, Bus or Taxi? for fares and times. Keeping your transit card in an outer pocket or phone case saves you from pulling off gloves at every gate; the card options are compared in T-money, Climate Card or WOWPASS?.

On snowy days, choose rail over road where you can. Buses and taxis slow down, and hilly neighborhoods such as the alleys around older parts of Seoul can be icy for a day or two after a snowfall. Intercity trains (KTX, SRT) are less affected than express buses. Flights at Incheon and Gimpo can see de-icing delays during heavy snow, so leave extra time on those days.

Common mistakes

  1. Packing for the average, not the cold snap. The January mean of -1.9°C sounds mild. The morning low of -5.5°C, and the -10°C mornings during a cold wave, are what you dress for.
  2. One huge coat, nothing underneath. Indoor heating is strong. Dress in layers you can remove on the subway or in a cafe.
  3. Assuming the south is warm. Busan and Jeju are milder, not warm. January highs around 8°C with sea wind still call for a real coat.
  4. Ignoring the wind. Seoul's winter wind from the northwest makes 0°C feel much colder. A windproof outer layer is more useful than a thicker sweater.
  5. Planning long outdoor afternoons. With sunset before 5:30 p.m. in December and January, palaces, villages and viewpoints are best in the late morning and early afternoon.
  6. Skipping the alert app. Alerts in Korean are easy to ignore or misread. The Emergency Ready App turns them into something you can act on.
  7. Forgetting skin care. Dry air plus heating means chapped lips and hands within a day or two. Buy balm locally if you forget it.
