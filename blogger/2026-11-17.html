Jjimjilbang Guide: How to Use a Korean Sauna, Etiquette and Rules for First-Timers

Short answer: A jjimjilbang (찜질방) is a Korean bathhouse with a large, clothed sauna floor attached. You pay once at the counter, receive a locker key, a small towel and a cotton T-shirt-and-shorts set, bathe without clothing in the gender-separated bath area, then put on the outfit and move to the shared floor with heated rooms, a snack counter and places to lie down. The three rules that matter most: wash thoroughly before entering any tub, keep towels and hair out of the water, and never take photos in the changing or bathing areas. Fees and hours are set by each facility, so check the sign at the door.

What a jjimjilbang is, and how it differs from a mogyoktang

Two words come up at the counter. A mogyoktang (목욕탕) is a bathhouse: showers, tubs and a sweat room, used naked and separated by gender. A jjimjilbang adds a shared floor where men and women sit together in the provided outfit. The Korea Tourism Organization explains the name through the old idea of sweating out illness, and describes a typical jjimjilbang as having multiple sauna rooms built from different materials, such as mud, charcoal, and salt, and set to temperatures ranging from 40℃ to 80℃.

Many places sell two tickets: bath only, or bath plus the jjimjilbang floor. The second one costs more and normally includes the outfit. If you only say "sauna," staff may hand you the cheaper bath-only ticket, so say "jjimjilbang" clearly if you want the full floor.

Step by step: from the door to the sauna floor

  1. Shoes off at the entrance. Put them in a shoe locker and take that key with you. In some places the shoe locker comes before the counter, in others after.
  2. Pay at the counter. After paying, you are given a dry towel, T-shirt, shorts, and a key for a locker. The key is usually on a wristband. In many facilities it also works as a tab: food and extras are recorded on it and paid when you leave. Keep it on your wrist the whole time.
  3. Find your gender's locker room. The locker number matches your key. Undress completely. Swimwear and underwear are not worn in the bath area.
  4. Wash first. Sit at a washing station (low stool, hand shower, bowl) or use a standing shower and wash with soap and shampoo from head to toe. According to the Korea Tourism Organization, bath supplies are not provided, although guests can purchase single-use shampoo, hair conditioner and other bath products within the jjimjilbang. Some places do have communal soap, but do not count on it.
  5. Use the tubs and sweat rooms. There are usually hot, warm and cold tubs plus a dry sauna or steam room inside the bath area. Go at your own pace.
  6. Dry off completely in the changing area, then put on the T-shirt and shorts.
  7. Move to the shared floor. Heated rooms, the heated ondol floor, snacks, TV and sleeping areas are here. You can go back to the bath area later; many people wash again before leaving.
  8. Leaving. Change into your own clothes, drop the outfit and towels in the return bin, settle any tab at the counter, hand back the key and collect your shoes.

Bathing-area etiquette

These habits are shared across generations, though older regulars tend to be stricter about them than younger visitors.

  • Wash before every entry into a tub, not just the first time. The tub water is shared.
  • Keep your towel out of the water. Tie long hair up so it does not float in the tub.
  • Rinse your stool and bowl after using a washing station, and return them to where you found them.
  • No splashing, swimming or loud talking. Keep the hand shower pointed at yourself.
  • Phones stay in the locker. Photos in changing rooms and bath areas are a serious violation of other people's privacy, and staff will intervene.
  • The body scrub (seshin, 세신) is an optional paid service done by staff at a table in the bath area. Ask for the price before you lie down.
  • Tattoos: the legal list of people a bathhouse may refuse does not mention tattoos, but individual facilities can post their own restrictions. If you have large tattoos, check the signs at the entrance or ask.

The shared floor: rooms, heat and food

The clothed floor is where the jjimjilbang gets its character. Typical rooms include a very hot kiln-style room (bulgama), a yellow-clay room, a salt room, a charcoal room and an ice room. Each room has its temperature posted at the door. Between rooms, people lie on the heated floor, eat and nap.

Pace yourself. A Korea.kr government policy briefing on jjimjilbang use warns that if you are exposed to high heat for too long, your strength drops quickly and fatigue builds, blood pressure rises and the heart is strained; in general a single session should not exceed 30 minutes, and if you feel tired or worn out you should come out right away. The same briefing says not to use a jjimjilbang after drinking alcohol, and that going to one to cure a hangover is off-limits. It also advises against stepping straight into cold wind after leaving the jjimjilbang.

The snack counter sells sikhye, kiln-baked eggs and simple meals. The folded towel hat you see on people's heads is called yangmeori ("sheep's head"); it keeps your hair out of the way and is half the fun. In the sleeping rooms, keep quiet and keep your phone on silent.

Rules written into Korean law

Bathhouses in Korea are regulated under the Public Health Control Act and the hygiene standards in Annex 4 of its Enforcement Rule. A few of those rules affect travellers directly.

  • Children and the opposite-sex bath. The standards state that bathing rooms and changing rooms must not admit males and females aged 4 (48 months) or older together. This limit was lowered from age 5 in a Ministry of Health and Welfare amendment that took effect on 22 June 2022. In practice, a father cannot take his five-year-old daughter into the men's bath, and a mother cannot take her five-year-old son into the women's bath. The clothed shared floor is fine for families of any mix.
  • Teenagers late at night. The current rule says that a jjimjilbang-type bathhouse operating 24 hours must restrict entry by youths (as defined in the Youth Protection Act) during the hours, within the range of 10 p.m. to 5 a.m., set by the provincial or metropolitan governor. Seoul district health centres publish the window as 22:00–05:00; Songpa-gu, for example, lists entry allowed 05:00–22:00 and barred 22:00–05:00 for bathhouses with jjimjil facilities. The main exceptions are being accompanied by a parent or guardian, or holding a written entry-consent form from a parent or guardian stating the youth's details, the reason and permitted time of entry, and the guardian's details and signature. Under the Youth Protection Act "youth" means a person under 19, excluding those who have reached 1 January of the year they turn 19. If you are under 19 or look like you might be, carry your passport.
  • Intoxicated visitors. The standards list, among people who must not be admitted, those judged unable to use the bathhouse normally because of drinking or similar reasons.
  • Health warnings in sweat rooms. Operators must post a caution notice at the sweat-room entrance covering children who have a cold, are under age 5 or show general weakness; people with a systolic blood pressure of 180 mmHg or higher; and people at risk of cataracts or with facial flushing. If you have a heart or blood-pressure condition, treat that notice as a reason to talk to your own doctor before a trip, not as a checklist to pass.

Spending the night in a jjimjilbang

Many large jjimjilbangs run 24 hours, and some charge a higher night rate; the amount varies by facility. Sleeping means a mat and a wooden block pillow on a heated floor in a shared room, with lights dimmed rather than off, and some snoring. It is a reasonable fallback for a late arrival or an early flight, and in winter it is a warm one. It is not a hotel: there are no beds, no check-out time, and by regulation no private rooms, since the facility standards require that sweat rooms, convenience facilities and rest rooms (including sleeping rooms) be visible from outside and not built as closed rooms. Two things to confirm before relying on it: that the specific facility really does operate overnight (plenty close in the evening, and some well-known ones such as Seoul's Dragon Hill Spa have shut down altogether), and that nobody in your group falls under the late-night minor rule above. For what winter nights feel like outside, see Korea in Winter: How Cold It Gets and What to Pack.

Common first-timer mistakes

  • Wearing a swimsuit into the bath area. It marks you out immediately and staff may ask you to remove it.
  • Stepping into a tub without washing. This is the one that annoys regulars most.
  • Going straight to the hottest room. Start with a warm room, rest, then try the kiln.
  • Staying in a hot room past the point of comfort because others are still there. Leave when you feel light-headed.
  • Walking onto the shared floor still dripping. Dry off properly in the changing room first.
  • Taking the key wristband off and leaving it by a tub.
  • Arriving after a long dinner with soju. Heat and alcohol are a bad mix, and the facility can turn you away.
  • Assuming every jjimjilbang has a sleeping room, English signs or 24-hour hours. Larger city facilities often do; neighbourhood ones often do not.
