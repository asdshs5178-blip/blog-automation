Korean Drinking Etiquette: Pouring Rules, Soju Basics and How to Say No Politely

Short answer: At a Korean table, you pour for other people and let them pour for you, you use two hands (or support your pouring arm) when the other person is older or senior, and you can decline a drink without causing a problem as long as you do it warmly. The legal drinking age works by birth year, not birthday: in 2026, anyone born in 2007 or earlier can buy alcohol, and a passport counts as ID. The drink-driving limit is a blood alcohol concentration of 0.03 percent, which one or two glasses of soju can reach. The rest of this post explains the pouring customs, what is actually in the bottles, polite ways to say no, and the few laws visitors ask about most.

The two rules that matter most

If you remember nothing else, remember these two. First, do not fill your own glass. In a group, someone will notice your empty glass and fill it, and you are expected to do the same for others. Watching for empty glasses is the whole social mechanic. Second, when the person pouring or receiving is older than you or senior to you at work, use two hands. The common forms are holding the bottle with your right hand and resting your left hand lightly on your right forearm, or holding the glass with both hands when it is being filled. Between friends of the same age, one hand is normal and nobody will think twice.

A third custom you will see in older or more formal groups: when you drink in front of someone clearly senior, turn your head slightly to the side and drink facing away. It looks odd the first time, but it is a small gesture and it is relaxing among younger Koreans. Speaking as a Korean, I would say the two-hands habit survives much more strongly than the head turn, especially when a boss or a grandparent is at the table.

How a round at the table usually goes

Here is the typical sequence at a dinner where soju is on the table. Nothing here is a strict ritual; it is simply what most groups do without thinking about it.

  1. The first bottle is opened and the youngest person, or the host, pours for the most senior person first, then around the table.
  2. Someone pours yours. Lift your glass slightly off the table, with two hands if they are older, and a quiet "gamsahamnida" (thank you) is enough.
  3. Take the bottle and pour for the person who poured for you, if their glass is empty. Do not top up a glass that still has drink in it; wait until it is empty.
  4. The first glass is often drunk together after "geonbae" (cheers). After that, people drink at their own pace.
  5. Keep an eye on glasses near you. Refilling an empty glass for someone is friendly, not pushy.
  6. Soju glasses are small. Nobody counts how much you drink, but an untouched full glass signals that you do not want more, which is useful later.

Food is part of this. Korean bars and pubs expect you to order "anju," dishes eaten with drinks, and many places expect at least one dish per table. Drinking on an empty stomach is unusual here.

How to decline a drink without offending anyone

Nobody is required to drink in Korea, and plenty of Koreans do not. The awkwardness, when it happens, comes from the pouring custom: a person offering to fill your glass is being friendly, so a flat "no" can land harder than you intend. The fix is to decline the drink while accepting the gesture.

  • Keep your glass full. A full glass does not get refilled. Accept the first pour, take a small sip or none, and leave it. This is the quietest way to stop drinking and it works at most tables.
  • Accept the pour, then do not drink it. Receiving with two hands and setting the glass down is polite even if you never touch it. Covering the glass with your hand is understood too, though a smile helps.
  • Give a short reason. "I have to drive," "I am on medication," "I do not drink for religious reasons," or "I have an early morning" are all accepted without follow-up in most company. You do not owe details.
  • Ask for something else in the glass. Cider (Korean lemon-lime soda), cola or water in your glass lets you join every "geonbae" without alcohol. Many people do exactly this.
  • Offer to pour instead. If you are not drinking, being the person who refills others' glasses keeps you part of the table.

If someone keeps pressing after a clear, friendly no, that is on them, not you. It is less common than it used to be, and younger colleagues in particular tend to leave it alone. Legal points visitors ask about

Drinking age and ID checks

Korea's Youth Protection Act defines a "youth" as anyone under 19, but excludes people who have reached January 1 of the year they turn 19. In practice, eligibility is by birth year. For 2026 that means people born in 2008 or later are still covered by the act, while anyone born in 2007 became eligible on January 1 (the cutoff moves forward by one year each January 1, so this line needs updating in 2027). The act also requires anyone selling alcohol to check the customer's age and identity, and local authorities tell shops that the check must be done with an official ID such as a passport, driver's licence or resident registration card, not a photo on a phone, and that an expired ID does not count. So carry your passport if you expect to be checked. Under the Food Sanitation Act, a shop or bar caught serving a minor can face a business suspension, which is why staff are strict about it.

Drink driving, including bicycles

Article 44(4) of the Road Traffic Act sets the limit at a blood alcohol concentration of 0.03 percent, in force since June 25, 2019, replacing the 0.05 percent standard Korea had kept since 1962. The government's own policy briefing described 0.03 percent as roughly what one or two glasses of soju produce. The same article says nobody may drive a motor vehicle, tram or bicycle while drunk, so a rented bike after a drink is not a loophole; the penalties for bicycles are lighter than for cars, but 0.03 percent is still the enforcement line. Refusing a breath test is itself an offense. Take a taxi or the subway.

Drinking outdoors

There is no nationwide ban on drinking in public, and you will see people with beer in parks and at the Han River in warm months. However, a 2020 amendment to the National Health Promotion Act lets each local government designate "alcohol-free zones" by ordinance and fine anyone caught drinking there up to 100,000 won. Seoul's city park ordinance sets the fine at 100,000 won for drinking in a designated zone, and districts have begun using it: Gwangjin-gu, for example, designated a children's park as its first alcohol-free zone and started enforcement in January 2024. Amounts differ elsewhere; Goyang in Gyeonggi-do set its fine at 50,000 won for its children's parks and playgrounds. The Han River parks themselves were not a designated zone as of this writing: a 2023 Seoul proposal to include the river was shelved by the city council for lack of public consensus, and I found no designation notice since. Designations change, so look for signs and follow them. Drinking on the subway or bus is not acceptable regardless of signs.

Common mistakes and how much this varies

  • Pouring your own drink. Harmless among friends, but at a work dinner or with an older host, hand the bottle to someone instead.
  • Topping up a half-full glass. Wait for it to be empty. Refilling early can read as pushing someone to drink faster.
  • Treating soju like beer. It is a spirit, poured in small glasses for a reason. Somaek hides the alcohol well.
  • Assuming you must match the table. Nobody needs to keep pace. A full glass and a smile cover you.
  • Forgetting the bill. Splitting is common among friends; a host or the most senior person often pays at work dinners. See our tipping guide for how paying works, and note there is no tip.

How strictly any of this applies depends on who is at the table. A group of university students in Hongdae will pour one-handed, skip the head turn and think nothing of it. A company dinner with a director present will be noticeably more formal. Family gatherings in smaller towns can go either way. Watch what the people around you do for the first few minutes and copy them; that is what Koreans do in an unfamiliar group too.
