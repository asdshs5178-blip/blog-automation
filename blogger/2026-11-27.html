Google Maps in Korea: Transit Works, No Walking Directions Yet, and What to Use

Short answer: Google Maps works in Korea for finding places, reading reviews and planning bus and subway trips, but it still does not give turn-by-turn walking or driving directions. The government approved Google's map data export in February 2026 and confirmed in November 2026 that Google met the security conditions, and reports say walking and driving directions could arrive in early 2027. Until that actually launches, install Naver Map (English interface, English voice navigation) as your main map, KakaoMap as a backup, and k.ride or Kakao T for taxis. This post explains what each app does, how to set them to English, and what to prepare before you land.

What Google Maps can and cannot do in Korea

Korean law restricts taking detailed map data (more precise than 1:25,000 scale) out of the country without approval, and Google's servers are overseas. That is why Google Maps in Korea has had a strange half-working feel for years. As of late November 2026, here is the practical picture.

The gap that hurts most is walking. Coming out of a subway exit and finding a cafe three alleys away is exactly the moment Google Maps leaves you with a straight dotted line. A Korean map app draws the actual turns.

What is changing, and when

On 27 February 2026, the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport conditionally approved Google's request to export 1:5,000-scale map data, the first time a foreign company received such approval. The conditions included removing coordinates for Korean territory and masking security facilities. On 13 November 2026, the ministry confirmed that Google had met those conditions, with a domestic partner processing the data before transfer.

Google has said its plan is a full-functioning Maps service in Korea including turn-by-turn directions, and Korean media reported that walking and driving directions could appear as early as the beginning of 2027. Google has not published a launch date. Treat it as planned, not done: if you are traveling in the next few months, pack a Korean map app.

Naver Map: the main map for most visitors

Naver Map (네이버 지도, the green icon, not the main Naver search app) is the map app I use daily, and it is the one with the most complete foreign-language support. The app runs in Korean, English, Simplified Chinese and Japanese, offers English voice navigation for driving, and translates Korean user reviews into English, Chinese and Japanese with Naver's Papago engine. Naver also publishes a multilingual usage guide for visitors, which has been available since December 2024.

To switch the app to English:

  1. Install "NAVER Map, Navigation" from the App Store or Google Play. Basic search and directions do not need an account or a Korean phone number.
  2. Open the profile or menu tab (labeled MY in recent versions), then Settings, then Language.
  3. Choose English. The app reloads in English; menus, transit instructions and most station names change, but some shop names and reviews stay in Korean.

Two tips. First, search by the Korean name or the phone number when an English name returns nothing; small restaurants are often registered only in Korean, so copy the Korean name from the shop's Instagram or website. Second, the English setting exists only in the mobile app. The desktop site is Korean-only, so plan on your phone, not a laptop.

One useful 2026 change: since 4 June 2026, Naver offers passport verification for foreigners. According to Naver's announcement, a visitor with a foreign-issued passport can verify identity on a smartphone and then use Naver Map reservations, ordering and payment without a Korean mobile number. This matters for popular restaurants that only take bookings through Naver. Availability depends on the individual shop, so check whether the listing shows a booking button.

KakaoMap: a solid second map

KakaoMap (카카오맵) covers the same ground: walking, driving, bus and subway routes, real-time bus arrivals and a street-level "Road View". The app store listing documents English and Korean, and the language is changed in the app settings menu. Sources disagree on whether Chinese and Japanese are also offered, so I have left that out. For an English-only reader, Naver Map's translation features are more complete, but KakaoMap is worth keeping for a second opinion when a place is missing or a route looks odd. Neither app requires a Korean phone number for search and directions.

Taxi apps: Kakao T, k.ride and Uber

Most taxis in Korea are dispatched through Kakao Mobility's network. The company runs two apps that reach it.

If you arrive with a foreign number and a foreign card, k.ride is the simpler starting point: the interface is in English, Japanese and Chinese, destination search and driver chat are auto-translated, and the fare estimate shows before you call. Kakao T offers more taxi types and services but was built around Korean identity verification, so its in-app card registration is where visitors most often get stuck. If you do use Kakao T, look for the direct-payment option before you confirm the request, and carry some cash or a transit card. See the T-money and WOWPASS comparison for how those cards work in a taxi.

Whatever app you use, check the plate number and the driver photo against the screen before getting in, and sign up before you swap your home SIM for a Korean data-only eSIM, since verification codes go to the number you registered.

Other apps worth installing

  • VISITKOREA, the official app of the Korea Tourism Organization. It carries attraction, festival and transport information in English and several other languages, a travel planner, and a link to the 1330 travel helpline. Details on the helpline are in the emergency numbers post.
  • Papago, Naver's translation app. Its camera mode handles menus and signs, and Korean-English quality is good for everyday phrases. Google Translate also works in Korea.
  • KakaoTalk, the messenger most people here use. Some guesthouses and small tour operators prefer it for contact, but a short-trip visitor can often skip it.

The Korea Tourism Organization keeps an official Helpful Apps and Resources page that lists these and a few more.

Set-up order before you fly

  1. Install Naver Map and switch it to English. Save your hotel by searching its Korean name or address and tapping the bookmark icon.
  2. Install KakaoMap as a backup and set it to English.
  3. Install k.ride (or Kakao T if you already have a Kakao account), complete sign-up and add a card while you still have your home phone number active.
  4. Install Papago and download the Korean language pack for offline use.
  5. Keep Google Maps for its reviews and for transit, and test whether walking directions have appeared; if they have, this post is out of date on that point.
  6. Plan your first ride from the airport with the Incheon Airport to Seoul guide, so you are not learning the apps and the AREX at the same time.

Common mistakes

  • Installing the Naver search app instead of Naver Map. Navigation lives in the separate green map app.
  • Searching English names only. Big landmarks work in English; a back-alley noodle shop usually needs its Korean name.
  • Signing up for a taxi app at the curb. Sign-up and card checks can take time; do it at home.
  • Assuming "approved" means "launched". Google Maps walking and driving directions were approved in principle in 2026 but had not launched as of this writing.
  • Relying on one app only. Place data differs between apps, and a second map takes thirty seconds to check.
