Korean Table Manners: Spoon for Rice, Bowls Stay Down, and Who Eats First

Short answer: At a Korean meal, use the spoon for rice and soup and the chopsticks for side dishes, keep your bowls on the table instead of lifting them, and if there is an older person or a host at the table, wait for them to pick up their spoon before you start. Side dishes (banchan) are shared, so take a little at a time. In restaurants, press the call button or say "jeogiyo" to get staff, and look for a self-service water station before asking. None of this is enforced strictly with visitors, and how formal things are depends a lot on who you are eating with. Below is what I was taught growing up here, checked against the guidance published by the Korea Heritage Service, the Korean Food Promotion Institute and the Korea Tourism Organization.

The basic table setting: what goes where

The Korea Heritage Service describes the traditional layout like this: the rice bowl sits on the diner's left, the soup bowl on the right, the spoon to the right of the soup, and the chopsticks to the right of the spoon. The same article says the proper way to begin is with a spoonful of soup or kimchi broth before anything else. The Korea Tourism Organization's food guide puts it more simply: the arrangement follows certain principles, and first, rice and soup, along with spoon and chopsticks, go directly in front of the diner; beyond that sits the main dish, with seafood, meat and vegetable dishes arranged around it.

In practice, a casual restaurant will just put the bowls down wherever there is room, and nobody will correct you if you move things. The layout matters more at a formal dinner or a family meal with grandparents. The one thing worth remembering is that your rice and soup are personal, while almost everything else on the table is for sharing.

Spoon and chopsticks: which one does what

Korean chopsticks are usually flat stainless steel, and they come with a long-handled metal spoon. The pair is called sujeo. The division of work is simple:

Use the spoon forUse the chopsticks for
Rice, soup, stew, porridge, anything with liquidSide dishes, meat, vegetables, noodles, kimchi

A few habits that go with this:

  • Do not hold the spoon and the chopsticks in the same hand at the same time. The Korean Food Promotion Institute lists this among its 12 rules for during the meal, right after the rule that the senior person picks up their spoon first. Put one down before you pick up the other.
  • Keep bowls on the table. Lifting the rice or soup bowl to your mouth is normal in some neighbouring food cultures, but in Korea it reads as sloppy. The same 12 rules include not lifting the soup bowl to drink from it. Bring the spoon to the bowl, not the bowl to your face.
  • Eat rice with the spoon, not the chopsticks. Metal chopsticks are not good at it anyway.
  • Never stand chopsticks or a spoon upright in a bowl of rice. That is how rice is offered at memorial rites for the dead, so it looks jarring at a dinner table.
  • When you pause, rest the chopsticks flat on the table, on a chopstick rest if there is one, or across the rim of a dish.

If chopsticks are hard for you, ask for a fork. Many restaurants have them, and nobody minds.

Who starts first, and how to pace yourself

The rule most Koreans learn as children is that the oldest person, or the guest of honour, picks up their spoon first and everyone else follows. The Korea Heritage Service puts it as: when eating with an elder or a guest, start when they pick up their spoon, and take care to match the elder's pace. If you unavoidably finish early, rest your spoon on your rice bowl, soup bowl or scorched-rice-water bowl, then put it down once the elder has finished. Putting your spoon down on the table means the meal is over, which is why during the meal it rests on the rice or soup bowl.

How much of this applies to you depends on the setting. With friends your own age, people just start eating. At a company dinner or a meal hosted by someone older, a short pause until the host starts is still a good idea. If you are the guest, your host may actually wait for you, so if nothing is happening, a smile and a "go ahead" gesture solves it.

Sharing banchan and other common dishes

Banchan are the small side dishes that arrive before or with your main order. They belong to the whole table, not to the person sitting closest to them.

  1. Take a small amount at a time rather than piling one side dish onto your plate.
  2. Pick from the top without digging around with your chopsticks.
  3. If serving utensils are provided, use them. The Korean Food Promotion Institute's rules include not putting utensils that have food on them into a dish everyone is eating from. The Korea Heritage Service adds that at a shared table you should move food onto a side plate, and pass banchan on a plate too. At a relaxed table of friends, people do reach in with their own chopsticks, so watch what the group is doing.
  4. If one pot of stew comes for the table and you would rather not share, ask for an extra bowl (ap-jeopsi, a small plate, or geureut, a bowl). This is a normal request.
  5. It is fine to mix a bit of rice, a side dish and some soup in one bite. The Korea Tourism Organization says you choose which foods to combine with each spoonful of rice, and there is no problem eating two or three things at once.

How restaurants actually work: call bells, water, refills

This is where visitors most often feel lost, because the system expects you to act rather than wait.

SituationWhat usually happens
Getting staffPress the call button on the table if there is one. If not, say "jeogiyo" (excuse me) in a normal voice. Waving silently tends not to work.
WaterOften a bottle or jug is brought, but many casual places post a sign saying water is self-service. Look for a dispenser and a cup steriliser cabinet.
Utensils and napkinsCheck for a drawer built into the side of the table before asking.
Side dish refillsThe Korea Tourism Organization states that at Korean restaurants there is no need to tip, requests for extra water or side dishes do not incur additional charges, and more and more restaurants are adopting self-service systems for water and side dishes, or unmanned ordering machines. Policies vary by restaurant and by dish, so ask rather than assume.
OrderingSome places use a tablet on the table or a kiosk at the entrance. Others still take orders at the table.
PayingUsually at the counter near the door, not at the table. Tipping is not expected.

I wrote more about paying and the no-tip habit in Tipping in Korea. Leaving a lot of refilled banchan untouched is the thing most likely to get you a frown, because it goes to waste. Take what you will eat.

Things that can come across as rude

  • Chopsticks or spoon standing upright in rice (see above).
  • Blowing your nose at the table. Step outside or go to the restroom.
  • Clinking the spoon against the bowl or tapping chopsticks on the table. The Korean Food Promotion Institute quotes Sasojeol, a 1700s etiquette text by Yi Deok-mu, which says not to tap the tray with chopsticks or let the spoon clink against the bowl, however hot the rice or soup, and not to blow on it.
  • Loud slurping. Traditional etiquette frowns on it, and noodles here are generally eaten more quietly than in some other countries, although a bit of noise is not a crisis.
  • Talking with a full mouth. Chatting itself is fine; most meals are lively.
  • Getting up and leaving while others are still eating, especially elders. If you must, say so.
  • Blowing on hot food to cool it. This is in the same old text and still in the Institute's 12 rules, and it is the one I see people ignore most. Treat it as formal-dinner advice only.

How strict is this really? Generations and situations

Be careful with any guide that says "all Koreans" do something. My own family is a good example: my grandparents' table had every rule above, my parents kept most of them, and my friends keep maybe half. Younger people often lift a bowl to drink the last of a broth at a noodle shop, and nobody blinks. The formality rises with the age gap at the table and with the occasion.

A rough guide:

  • Casual meal with friends or other travellers: spoon and chopsticks roles, no upright chopsticks, share politely. That is enough.
  • Meal hosted by a Korean family or someone older: add waiting for them to start, matching their pace, and using both hands when passing or receiving something.
  • Work or business dinner: all of the above, plus the drink-pouring customs. Those are covered in Korean Drinking Etiquette, including how to decline alcohol.
  • Temple meals or a temple stay: a separate and quieter set of rules, usually explained on the spot.

Restaurants differ too: the number of banchan, whether meat is grilled for you or by you, and how formal the place feels all change from one neighbourhood to the next.
